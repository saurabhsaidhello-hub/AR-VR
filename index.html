Worlds You
Step Into

A textbook can tell you how the Roman Forum looked. A film can show you. An AR/VR experience lets you stand in it, turn your head, and decide where to walk.

That shift from watching to being there changes how ideas must be told. This guide shows how to turn a concept and a story into something a person can actually experience.

( The core idea )

In AR/VR the user is not the audience. The user is the main character.

A concept is the idea you want people to understand or feel, like "coral reefs are dying" or "empathy for a wheelchair user." A narrative is the path through that idea: a beginning, a change, and a consequence. Implementation means choosing what the user sees, does, and feels at each step.

( Translating concepts )

Every abstract idea needs a physical, felt equivalent the user can touch or move through.

Ask three questions for each part of your concept: What should the user perceive? What should they do? What should they feel? The answers become your design.

( Narrative structures for 3D space )

Unlike a film, you cannot control where the user looks. So stories in AR/VR use spatial storytelling:

01 — Linear guided
The story moves one step at a time, with the user nudged along by sound, light, or a guide character. Best for first-time users and short lessons.
02 — Branching
User choices lead to different outcomes. Good for ethics, history, or decision training, but costly to build.
03 — Environmental
No fixed order. Story hides in objects, sounds, and places the user explores. Best for mystery and discovery.
04 — Embodied
The user plays a role (patient, refugee, pilot) and the story is what they feel in that role.
( Guiding attention )

Use light (brighter things draw the eye), sound (3D audio from the direction of the next event), motion, and character gaze. If a user looks away from the key moment, the world should pull them back gently, not break the story.

( What goes wrong )

The tech is impressive. The user still has to be comfortable, and have a reason to care.

Motion sickness
Avoid moving the camera without the user's own movement. Use teleport steps, fixed cockpits, or vignettes.
Tech over story
If you removed the headset and the idea is still unclear, the experience is a demo, not a narrative.
Overload
Too many glowing objects means nobody knows where to look. Start with one clear point of focus.
Ignoring access
Not everyone can stand, turn, or use hand controllers. Offer seated mode, captions, and alternate inputs.
Long sessions
Aim for 5 to 15 minutes. Fatigue is real and comfort is part of the story.

( Try it yourself )
01 — Concept to storyboard
Pick a concept from class (photosynthesis, democracy, gravity). Sketch six frames showing what the user sees and does, from a first-person view.
02 — Attention map
Draw a top-down room with a story event. Mark how light, sound, and motion will guide the user toward it.
03 — Role swap
Rewrite a textbook paragraph as an embodied story: who is the user, what do they want, what blocks them?
04 — Paper prototype
Act out your experience with a classmate as the user, eyes closed, while you describe the world aloud. Where do they get lost?
( The big idea )

Good AR/VR does not show people a story. It places them inside one and trusts them to care.

Start with the concept, find the human story inside it, then build only the space and interactions that story needs.

Now go build a world worth stepping into.
